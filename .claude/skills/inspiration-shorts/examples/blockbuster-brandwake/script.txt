Blockbuster collected seven hundred ninety-six million dollars in late fees in the year two thousand. Sixteen percent of everything it earned. Its filings call them extended viewing fees: rentals kept past the due date. Myth number one: Netflix was born from a forty dollar late fee. Reed Hastings told that story for years. His co-founder, Marc Randolph, has called it a convenient fiction, CNBC reported. What is on record: in September nineteen ninety-nine Netflix launched a subscription with no due dates and no late fees. Its own filing says so. Myth number two: late fees killed Blockbuster. Check the numbers. Late fees brought in seven hundred twenty-two million in two thousand three. Six hundred twenty-two million in two thousand four. On January first, two thousand five, it ended them in its stores. Its own filing says that cut rental revenue by more than five hundred million dollars. Forty-seven states and D.C. later settled over how the ads described it. The slogan said no late fees. The catch: keep a movie more than seven days and you could be charged its price. But fees were not the only wound. Blockbuster split from Viacom in two thousand four, with a special dividend paid for by borrowing. It carried about one point one billion dollars of debt. In September twenty ten, it filed for Chapter eleven. Verdict: late fees were a big piece of the business, and ending them was costly. One reason, not the only one. That is my read.
