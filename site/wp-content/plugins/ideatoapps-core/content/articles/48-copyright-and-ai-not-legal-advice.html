Can I use what an AI tool made for me? Can I give it someone else's work? Do I have to tell people? Creators who use AI tools ask these three questions often, and some parts of the answers are already clear.

This is not legal advice. [ita_term define="The legal right of a creator to control how their original work is copied, shared and changed, for a limited time."]Copyright[/ita_term] rules differ by country, and how they apply to AI is still changing. For anything involving a contract, a sale or a dispute, ask a qualified lawyer where you live.

Three questions, plain answers

Can I use what the tool made for me?

Start with the app's terms of use, the rules you agreed to when you signed up. Many apps say you may use what you generate, including in work you sell. Terms differ between apps, though, and they can change.

Being allowed to use something is different from owning it. In some places, copyright protects only work with enough human creative input. A cover image made entirely by a tool may then have weak or no protection. You could use it, but you couldn't stop others from copying it. The more you write, draw, edit and arrange yourself, the more of the work is clearly yours.

You're also responsible for what you publish. If a generated image looks like a well-known character or logo, leave it out.

[ita_why title="Where the open questions are"]

Courts and lawmakers in different countries are still working on several questions:

  • Whether training AI models on copyrighted work without permission is allowed.
  • How much human input a work needs before copyright protects it.
  • Who is responsible when generated output copies an existing work: the user, the app's maker, or both.
  • Which kinds of AI-made content must be labeled.

Answers may differ from one country to the next, so a rule you read about may not apply where you live.

[/ita_why]

Can I give it someone else's work?

You've probably wanted to paste a song you love and ask for "something like this". Your own work is fine to use. Someone else's work is different. Pasting it doesn't give you any right to publish what comes back, and the result may copy enough of the original to cause problems.

A general mood or style is a safer starting point than a named work. Asking a tool to copy a living artist's style raises fairness questions, and some apps' terms limit it. For music, images or text you bought or downloaded, check the [ita_term define="Written permission that says how you may use someone else's work."]license[/ita_term]. For a client's logo or photos, get the client's permission first.

Do I have to tell people?

Sometimes, yes. Some platforms ask you to label realistic images or video made with AI. Publishers, contests, schools and clients may have their own rules, and a contract can require you to say which parts used AI. Where no rule applies, the choice is yours, and a short note such as "cover art made with an AI tool" is easy to add.

Keep a record of how you made it

If a client, a platform or a contest asks how a piece was made, a short record helps you answer. A musician making a lyric video, or a marketing assistant building a brochure, can take short notes as they work. Each time you use an AI tool, save your [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] and the date.

At the end, paste your notes into this request to sort them into a record:

[ita_prompt title="Contribution record (example values: replace them)"] I'm making [a lyric video for my song]. Here are my notes on how I made it: [paste your notes] Sort them into a record under three headings: MADE BY ME: parts I wrote, recorded, filmed, drew or edited myself. MADE WITH AN AI TOOL: parts a tool generated, and what I changed afterward. FROM OTHER PEOPLE: music, images, clips or text by others, with where each came from and the permission or license, or UNKNOWN. Do not add anything that isn't in my notes. [/ita_prompt]

When to ask a lawyer

Some moments call for a conversation with a qualified lawyer, or with a creators' or trade organization that offers legal help. One is a contract that says who owns the work. Another is selling prints or products with generated art on them.

Ask too before you use a real person's face or voice. Separate rules from copyright cover that, and they also differ by country. If you receive a takedown notice or a claim against your work, get advice before you reply.

Try it

  1. Pick one recent piece you made with some AI help, such as a thumbnail, a caption, a script or a song cover.
  2. Write rough notes on how you made it: what you did, what the tool did and what you took from others.
  3. Run the contribution record request above on your notes.
  4. Open the terms of the app you used, find the part about who may use what you make, and note the date you read it.
  5. For any line marked UNKNOWN, find the source or replace that part before you publish.
