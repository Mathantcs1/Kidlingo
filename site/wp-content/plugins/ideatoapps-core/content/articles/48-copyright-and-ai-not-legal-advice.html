Can I use what an AI app made for me? Can I give it someone else's work? Do I have to tell people? Creators ask these questions a lot. Some answers are already clear, and some are still open.

This is not legal advice. [ita_term define="The legal right, usually first held by the creator or their employer, to control how an original work is copied, shared and changed, for a limited time."]Copyright[/ita_term] rules differ by country, and how they apply to AI is still changing. For anything involving a contract, a sale or a dispute, ask a qualified lawyer where you live.

Three questions, plain answers

Can I use what the app made for me?

Start with the app's terms of use, the rules you agreed to when you signed up. Many apps say you may use what you generate, including in work you sell. Terms differ between apps, though, and they can change.

Being allowed to use something is different from owning it. In some places, copyright protects only work with enough human creative input. A cover image made entirely by AI may then have weak or no protection. You could use it, but you might not be able to stop others from copying it. The more you write, draw, edit and arrange yourself, the more the work reflects your own creative choices. That matters in places that require human input.

You're also responsible for what you publish. If a generated image looks like a well-known character or logo, leave it out.

[ita_why title="Where the open questions are"]

Courts and lawmakers in different countries are still working on several questions:

  • Whether training AI models on copyrighted work without permission is allowed.
  • How much human input a work needs before copyright protects it.
  • Who is responsible when generated output copies an existing work: the user, the app's maker, or both.
  • Which kinds of AI-made content must be labeled.

Answers may differ from one country to the next, so a rule you read about may not apply where you live.

[/ita_why]

Can I give it someone else's work?

You've probably wanted to paste a song you love and ask for "something like this". Your own work is fine to paste, though the app's terms say what it may do with what you paste. Someone else's work needs more thought. Pasting it doesn't give you any right to publish what comes back, and the result may copy enough of the original to cause problems.

A general mood or style is a safer starting point than a named work. Asking an app to copy a living artist's style raises fairness questions, and some apps' terms limit it. For music, images or text you bought or downloaded, check the [ita_term define="Written permission that says how you may use someone else's work."]license[/ita_term]. For a client's logo or photos, get the client's permission first.

Do I have to tell people?

Sometimes, yes. Some platforms ask you to label realistic images or video made with AI. Publishers, contests, schools and clients may have their own rules, and a contract can require you to say which parts used AI. Where no platform rule, law or contract requires a label, the choice is yours. A short note like "cover art made with an AI app" is easy to add.

Keep a record of how you made it

If a client, a platform or a contest asks how a piece was made, a short record helps you answer. A musician making a lyric video can take short notes as they work. Each time you use an AI app, save your [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] and the date.

A marketing assistant who adds AI-made images to a company brochure needs a record too, because the employer has to answer for what it publishes. Keeping the notes in the project folder lets whoever takes over the brochure find them.

At the end, paste your notes into this request to sort them into a record:

[ita_prompt title="Contribution record"] I'm making [a lyric video for my song]. Here are my notes on how I made it: [paste your notes] Sort them into a record under three headings: MADE BY ME: parts I wrote, recorded, filmed, drew or edited myself. MADE WITH AN AI APP: parts an app generated, and what I changed afterward. FROM OTHER PEOPLE: music, images, clips or text by others, with where each came from and the permission or license, or UNKNOWN. Do not add anything that isn't in my notes. [/ita_prompt]

When to ask a lawyer

In some cases, talk to a qualified lawyer, or to a creators' or trade organization that offers legal help. One is a contract that says who owns the work. Another is selling prints or products with generated art on them.

Ask too before you use a real person's face or voice. Other rules, separate from copyright, cover faces and voices, and they also differ by country. If you receive a takedown notice or a claim against your work, get advice before you reply.

Try it

This takes about fifteen minutes.

  1. Choose one recent piece you made with some AI help, such as a thumbnail, a caption, a script or a song cover.
  2. Write rough notes on how you made it: what you did, what the app did and what you took from others.
  3. Run the contribution record request above on your notes.
  4. Open the terms of the app you used, find the part about who may use what you make, and note the date you read it.
  5. For any line marked UNKNOWN, find the source or replace that part before you publish.
