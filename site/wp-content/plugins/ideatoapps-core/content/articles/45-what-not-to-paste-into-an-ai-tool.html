Most of what people paste into AI apps is fine: a recipe, a draft email, a paragraph from their own essay. A few details should never go in, and some need trimming first. Sorting what you're about to paste into three groups takes a few seconds.

Fine to paste as it is

Your own writing with no private details in it is fine. So are general questions, public information and examples you made up. "Help me make this birthday invitation sound friendlier" needs no trimming at all.

It helps to know where your words go once you press send.

[ita_why title="Where your text goes after you send it"]

Unless the model runs on your own device, what you type goes to the provider's servers. It's processed there and usually stored for a time. How long depends on the provider and your settings, and a deleted chat may take a while to disappear from their systems.

In some services, people review a sample of chats, for example to check safety. Some may use chats to train later models unless you turn that off. If the app is connected to other services, such as email, parts of your request may go there too. The app's privacy page says what applies to you.

[/ita_why]

Remove details first

Health details, money details, information about your children and other people's messages all need care. You can usually get the same help without the personal parts. Use roles instead of names, describe a document instead of pasting it, and crop screenshots to the part you need.

It's late, you're filling in a school form, and one question about your child's allergy is hard to answer. Pasting the whole form would share your child's name, school and date of birth. Typing this instead gets the same help: "My nine-year-old has a mild nut allergy and carries medicine on school trips. Help me write two clear sentences for a school form."

At work, "Customer A" works as well as a real name, and the order number can usually stay out. Paste work documents only if your workplace allows it. Photos and screenshots can show more than you meant, such as a name in a notification or an address on a letter. Some photo files also store where the photo was taken.

Never paste these

These details can let someone into your accounts or let them pretend to be you. No app needs them to help you.

  • Passwords and codes. Passwords, PINs, one-time login codes, security answers and recovery codes.
  • Access keys. The long secret strings that let a program sign in to an account, such as those in a developer's settings file.
  • Full ID and account numbers. Passport, driver's license and national ID numbers, and bank card or account numbers.
  • Anything your workplace or school forbids. For example, confidential documents, customer lists or exam papers.

If you paste one of these by mistake, delete the chat and change the password or code. For a card or ID number, contact the bank or the office that issued it and ask what to do next.

Check your app's settings

Every app names its settings differently, but most have a privacy or data section. Look for these:

  • Whether your chats may be used to train or improve future models, and how to turn that off.
  • How long chats are kept, and how to delete them.
  • Whether memory is on. Memory adds saved details to later chats, and you can usually see and delete what it saved.
  • Whether there's a temporary chat mode that isn't saved to your history.
  • Which chats you've shared as links, since anyone with a link can usually open that chat.

At work, use the account your employer provides, if there is one. Work accounts often come with different terms from personal ones.

Try it

Do this with someone who uses an AI app, such as a parent, a partner or a coworker.

  1. Open the app's settings together and find the privacy or data section.
  2. Note what the settings for training, chat history and memory are set to.
  3. If the app has a memory list, read it together and delete anything that shouldn't be there.
  4. Pick one setting either of you wants changed, and change it now.
