What does this letter want me to do, and by when? For a long document, that question makes a better request than "summarize this". Ask for a summary built around it, then check the parts you'll act on against the original.

Before: a summary that helps nobody

A retired woman gets a long letter about the yearly review of her benefits. She pastes it into a chatbot and types "summarize this". The answer might look like this:

The letter explains the yearly review of your benefits. It describes the information the office needs, how payments are worked out, and your rights if you disagree. It also lists ways to contact the office.

Each sentence may be true, but she still doesn't know what to do. Does she have to send anything back? By when? What happens if she doesn't? A chatbot can't guess which parts matter to her, so it gives every topic a line.

After: a summary built around one decision

Before you write your [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term], decide what the summary is for. For this letter, the question is simple: what does it ask me to do, and by when? Ask for the details you'll act on to be copied word for word, and for each point to show where it came from.

If your app accepts files, attach the letter. If it doesn't, paste the letter at the end of this request:

[ita_prompt title="Action summary"] I'm [a retired person who receives these benefits]. Below is [a letter about the yearly review of my benefits]. Summarize it under four headings: WHAT I MUST DO: each action, with its deadline if there is one. WHAT HAPPENS IF I DON'T: only if the letter says so. WHAT I CAN SKIP: parts that don't apply to me, in one line. QUESTIONS FOR THE SENDER: anything unclear or missing. Copy every date, amount and condition word for word. After each point, name the section or page if the document shows one. Then quote the first few words of the sentence the point is based on. If a heading has nothing under it, write NOTHING FOUND. Do not give advice about money or benefits. [paste the letter] [/ita_prompt]

This time the answer might start with one action: send two named documents back by a date given on page 3.

Check it before you act on it

A summary is shorter because things were left out. Most of the time that's fine. The risk comes when a missing word changes what you should do.

For each point you'll act on, find the place in the letter, using the page the summary names. If you pasted the text, search the original for the quoted words instead. Check that the dates, amounts and conditions match the letter, and look for words like "unless", "except" and "only if". Page numbers can be wrong too, so if you can't find a point, treat it as unconfirmed.

For a letter about money, health or the law, the letter itself is what counts. Confirm anything unclear with the sender, using the phone number or address in the letter, or ask a qualified advisor.

An apprentice electrician with a long training manual has a different decision to make: which safety rules apply to tomorrow's job? They can ask for those rules one chapter at a time. Then they read each rule in the manual itself before the job starts.

Very long documents need one more step. An app may cut, shorten or only partly read a very long file (the article "Context windows" explains why). Summarize a long file one part at a time, then ask for one combined summary of your part summaries. Check the conditions against the parts, since each round of summarizing can drop more. Some apps also read only the text of a file, so check tables and scanned pages yourself.

[ita_why title="How a short version can change the meaning"]

Summaries tend to go wrong in a few ways, even when every sentence sounds right.

  • Conditions drop out. "Reply within 30 days unless you have already sent this form" becomes "Reply within 30 days."
  • Strength changes. "You may need to send proof of address" can become "Send proof of address", or the other way around.
  • Outside knowledge gets mixed in. The summary may add what offices like this usually ask for, even if this letter doesn't.
  • Long sections take more room. A long section can fill the summary while a short rule that matters more gets one line.

Try it

Use a long document you really need to act on, such as a letter from a service provider, a handbook or a work procedure. Take out names and account numbers first, and for work documents, use one only if your workplace allows it.

  1. Run the action summary request above on your document.
  2. Pick the two points you would act on first. Find each one in the original, using the page or the quoted words the summary gives.
  3. Next to each point, write "same", "missing a condition" or "not found".
