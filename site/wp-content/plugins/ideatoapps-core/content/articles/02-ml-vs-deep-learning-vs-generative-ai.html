Think of three boxes, one inside the next. Machine learning is the big box. Deep learning is a smaller box inside it. Generative AI mostly sits inside deep learning. So the three terms aren't rivals: each one is a narrower slice of the one before.

The labels get mixed up all the time, in news stories and on job ads alike. Knowing which box you're in helps you guess what a system needs, what it's good at and how it tends to fail.

Three nested boxes

Machine learning: learning from examples

[ita_term define="A way of building software where the program learns patterns from examples instead of following only rules a person wrote by hand."]Machine learning[/ita_term] (ML) is the broad idea. You don't write every rule yourself. You give the program many examples, and it adjusts itself until its guesses match them well.

Say you want to predict whether a library book will come back late. You could hand-write rules. Or you could give a program thousands of past loans, with details like the borrower's history and the due date, plus whether each one came back late. The program finds the patterns for you. Many methods do this, and plenty of them are quite simple.

Deep learning: many layers

[ita_term define="A kind of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers stacked on top of each other."]Deep learning[/ita_term] is one family of ML methods. It uses a [ita_term define="A model made of many simple connected units that pass numbers to each other and adjust their connections during training."]neural network[/ita_term] with many layers. Each layer turns its input into a slightly more useful form for the next one.

This works well for messy inputs such as photos, sound and free text, where writing good features by hand is hard. A deep network can learn for itself that certain shapes of pixels tend to mean "edge", then "eye", then "face". The cost is that it usually needs far more data and computing power than simpler methods.

Generative AI: making new content

[ita_term define="AI that produces new content, such as text, images, audio or code, instead of only sorting or scoring existing data."]Generative AI[/ita_term] is about output. Most ML predicts a label or a number: spam or not, late or on time. A generative model produces something new, like a paragraph, a picture or a short melody.

Today's generative systems are almost all built with deep learning. Chatbots run on a [ita_term define="A program trained on a very large amount of text to predict which piece of text is likely to come next."]language model[/ita_term], which writes by predicting the next piece of text again and again. Image tools often learn to turn random noise into a picture, step by step.

Side by side

Here's the same comparison as a quick reference. Treat it as a rough guide: real projects often mix methods.

  • Typical job. Classic ML: sort, score or forecast. Deep learning: recognize patterns in images, sound or text. Generative AI: produce new text, images or audio.
  • Typical input. Classic ML often works on tables, like rows in a spreadsheet. Deep learning shines on raw media and long text. Generative models are trained on very large collections of examples.
  • Data and computing needs. Classic ML can work with modest data on an ordinary laptop. Deep learning usually needs much more of both. Large generative models need the most, by a wide margin.
  • How it tends to fail. Classic ML can be thrown off by data that looks different from its training data. Deep learning can be hard to explain. Generative models can produce fluent output that is simply false.
[ita_why title="Why is deep learning harder to explain?"]

A simple model, such as a decision tree, makes its choice through a few readable questions: "Has this borrower returned late before? Is the loan longer than two weeks?" You can follow the path.

A deep network spreads what it learned across a huge number of numeric [ita_term define="The adjustable numbers inside a model that change during training and decide how it turns input into output."]parameters[/ita_term]. No single number means "late borrower". Researchers have methods to probe what a network pays attention to, but this area, often called interpretability, is still being worked on and debated.

[/ita_why]

Picking the right word

When you read or write about a system, ask what it outputs and how it was built. A few common cases:

  • A tool that flags fraud from transaction tables is ML. It may or may not use deep learning.
  • A photo app that finds faces is almost certainly deep learning. It isn't generative, because it labels pictures instead of making new ones.
  • A chatbot is generative AI, built with deep learning, which is a kind of ML. All three labels are true at once.

Builders face a practical version of the same question. Deep learning isn't automatically the right choice. For a small table of numbers, a simpler method is often faster to train, cheaper to run and easier to explain. For drafting text, a generative model fits. Starting from the job, instead of the trendiest label, saves time.

The lines also blur. Some people say "AI" when they mean any of these; others use "ML" only for the classic methods. If a term matters for a decision, ask what the system actually does.

That last line in the [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] matters. It asks the chatbot to show where its answer is uncertain, so you know what to check.

Try it

Spend five minutes sorting the AI you already meet.

  1. Write down four things you used this week that might involve AI. A spam filter, a map's travel time, a photo search and a chatbot are good examples.
  2. For each one, write its output: a label, a number or new content.
  3. Place each in a box: classic ML, deep learning, generative AI, or "not sure".
  4. Paste one "not sure" item into the prompt above and compare the chatbot's answer with yours. Note what detail it says would change its answer.

You won't always know how a product was built, and that's fine. The output alone usually tells you which box to start from.
