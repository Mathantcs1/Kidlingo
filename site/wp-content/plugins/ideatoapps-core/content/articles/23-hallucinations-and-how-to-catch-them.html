A common mistake is to trust a reading list or a reference because it looks complete. A chatbot can invent a book title, an author and a page number, and all three can look real. When a model states something false or made up as if it were true, it's called a [ita_term define="When an AI model states something false or made up as if it were true."]hallucination[/ita_term].

Why a chatbot makes things up

A chatbot writes what is likely to come next. It doesn't check facts unless the app adds search results or a file to the chat.

On a well-known topic, the likely text is often true. On a rare topic, the model still writes likely-looking text. It produces a typical-sounding name, a plausible title or a round number. Nothing inside it stops to say "I don't have this fact."

Training is one reason too. Models have often been rewarded more for giving an answer than for saying "I don't know." Developers are working on this. Still, don't expect a chatbot to say "I don't know" every time it should.

Search helps, but it doesn't remove the problem. A chatbot can misread a page, mix two pages together, or add details that no page contains.

Signs that a detail may be made up

You can't spot every hallucination by reading. Some signs make a check more urgent:

[ita_checklist id="made-up-detail-signs"] [ita_item]The answer gives very exact details on a niche topic: page numbers, dates, percentages or word-for-word quotes.[/ita_item] [ita_item]It lists sources you didn't give it, and you can't see search results in the chat.[/ita_item] [ita_item]A real author is linked to a book or article you have never heard of.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Names, numbers or dates change when you ask the same question in a new chat.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Your question assumed something, and the answer treats it as true.[/ita_item] [ita_item]It says it read a link or file that your app can't open.[/ita_item] [/ita_checklist]

If an app can't open web links, the chatbot may still "summarize" a linked page. It builds the summary from the words in the link and the rest of the chat.

Questions that invite made-up answers

Some questions make a hallucination more likely. A teacher or tutor can use them on purpose to show students the problem.

  • Questions with a false idea inside. Say your town's library has never won an award. Ask "Why did our library win a national award?" and a chatbot may explain the award.
  • Requests for an exact count. "Give me five sources" can push it to fill all five, even if it knows of two.
  • Questions about small details. The exact words of a speech, or the page where a quote appears, are rarely in the training text often enough for the model to get them exactly right.

Ask open questions instead: "Are there any studies on this?" leaves room for "I don't know of any." Then check the answer. A [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] can turn an answer into a list you can check, one line at a time:

[ita_prompt title="Make a checking sheet"] Here is an answer from a chatbot: [paste the answer] List every name, title, number, date, quote and source in it, one per line. Next to each one, write the words I could search for to find it. After each line, leave a blank for me to write "found" or "not found". Do not tell me whether any of them are true. [/ita_prompt]

Paste it into a new chat, so the sheet is made without the earlier conversation.

[ita_why title="What makes a fake reference look real"]

Training text contains a huge number of references. They all follow a few patterns: author, year, title, publisher or journal. A model learns these patterns very well.

So when it doesn't know a real source, it can still write something in the right shape. It may use a real journal name, a common surname and a title made of the topic's usual words. Every piece looks right, but the whole thing may not exist.

Try it

This works well with a student, a class or your own child.

  1. Pick a topic your student knows well from class.
  2. Ask a chatbot for a short paragraph on it with three sources, including authors and years.
  3. Run the checking-sheet prompt above on that paragraph.
  4. Before you share it, try to find each line yourself and note which ones you could find.
  5. Give the student the paragraph and the blank sheet, and ask them to mark each line "found" or "not found".
