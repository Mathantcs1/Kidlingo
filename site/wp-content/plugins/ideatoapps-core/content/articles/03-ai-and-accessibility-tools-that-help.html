AI now sits behind many of the features that help people see, hear, read and type: dictation, live captions, read-aloud voices, image descriptions and tools that make text simpler. They can save real effort every day. They also make mistakes, so it helps to know where each one is strong and where it slips.

Below, the features are grouped by the need they meet. Many phones and computers already include some of them in their settings, often under a heading such as "Accessibility".

Features grouped by need

Hearing: captions and transcripts

Automatic captions turn speech into text as it happens, in a video, a video call or a class. A transcript is the same idea written out in full afterwards. Both rely on [ita_term define="Software that turns spoken words into written text."]speech recognition[/ita_term], which is often built with machine learning.

Captions work well with one clear speaker in a quiet room. They tend to struggle with background noise, people talking over each other, strong accents, and unusual names or technical words. A caption that turns "no" into "know" is easy to spot. One that drops the word "not" can flip the meaning.

Seeing: screen readers, read-aloud and image descriptions

A [ita_term define="Software that reads out loud, or shows in braille, what is on a screen, so people can use a device without seeing it."]screen reader[/ita_term] speaks what is on the screen and lets a person move around a page by headings, links and buttons. [ita_term define="A feature that reads written text aloud in a computer voice."]Text-to-speech[/ita_term] reads text aloud, and AI has made many of these voices sound more natural.

Some apps can also describe a photo, or read the text in a picture of a sign or a letter. This can be a big help. But an automatic description can miss what matters, guess wrong about people, or misread a number.

Reading: simpler words and summaries

A chatbot can rewrite a dense letter in shorter sentences, explain a hard word, or turn a long page into a short list. This can help people with dyslexia, people learning a language, people who are tired or unwell, and anyone facing a wall of official text.

The risk is that a simpler version can leave out a detail or change it. For anything important, like a deadline, a dose or an amount owed, check the original.

Typing and moving: dictation and voice control

Dictation lets you speak instead of type. Voice control goes further and lets you open apps or press buttons by voice. These help people with limited hand movement, pain or tiredness, and anyone whose hands are busy. Like captions, dictation can mishear, so read the text back before you send it.

Where these features fall short

AI features are helpers. They can't fix a page or a lesson that wasn't built to be accessible in the first place.

A screen reader can only read what is there. If a document has no real headings, or a chart has no written description, the screen reader user misses out, and an automatic guess is a weak stand-in. Captions written or checked by a person are usually more accurate than automatic ones. An image description from the person who chose the image knows why the image is there; an automatic one doesn't.

So if you make materials for others, such as a teacher sharing slides or a parent group sending a newsletter, accessible design still comes first. Use real headings, write short descriptions for images, and give videos captions you've checked.

[ita_why title="Why do captions get some voices wrong more often?"]

Speech recognition learns from recordings of people talking, paired with the correct text. If some accents, ages or ways of speaking were less common in those recordings, the system has had less practice with them, and it tends to make more mistakes.

Noise and overlapping voices make the job harder for everyone. Rare names and specialist words are hard too, because the system is more likely to pick a common word that sounds similar.

[/ita_why]

A quick check before you rely on one

Whether you're setting something up for yourself, a student or a family member, a few minutes of testing helps.

[ita_checklist id="accessibility-feature-check"] [ita_item]Try the feature on something you already know well, so mistakes are easy to spot.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Test it in the real setting: the noisy classroom, the quiet bedroom, the busy bus.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Check names, numbers, dates and the word "not" in any captions or dictated text.[/ita_item] [ita_item]For important letters or forms, compare a simplified version with the original.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Ask the person who uses it what helps and what gets in the way.[/ita_item] [/ita_checklist]

To try simpler reading with a chatbot, you can give it a [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] like this one:

[ita_prompt title="Make a letter easier to read"] Rewrite the text below in short, plain sentences for someone who finds long text hard to read. Keep every date, amount and deadline exactly as written. At the end, list anything you left out. [paste the text here] [/ita_prompt]

Asking it to keep the key facts and to list what it left out makes missing details easier to catch. It isn't a guarantee, so still check the original for anything that matters.

Try it

Pick one feature from this article and test it for five minutes.

  1. Turn on dictation or captions on your phone or computer. Look in the settings if you can't find it.
  2. Read a short paragraph from a book aloud, including one name and one number.
  3. Compare what the device wrote with the book. Count the mistakes and note what kind they were.
  4. Try again somewhere noisier, or a little faster, and see what changes.

Now you know how much to trust that feature, and in which setting.
