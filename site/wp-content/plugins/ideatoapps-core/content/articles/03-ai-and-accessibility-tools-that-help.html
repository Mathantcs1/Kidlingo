AI now sits behind many features that help people see, hear, read and type. Examples include dictation, live captions, read-aloud voices, image descriptions and simpler text. They can save real effort every day. They also make mistakes, so it helps to know where each one is strong and where it slips.

Many phones and computers already include some of these features in their settings, often under a heading such as "Accessibility".

Features grouped by need

Hearing: captions and transcripts

Automatic captions turn speech into text as it happens, in a video, a video call or a class. A transcript is the same idea written out in full afterward. Both rely on [ita_term define="Software that turns spoken words into written text."]speech recognition[/ita_term], which is often built with machine learning.

Captions work well with one clear speaker in a quiet room. They tend to struggle with background noise and with people talking over each other. Some accents, unusual names and technical words cause trouble too. A caption that turns "no" into "know" is easy to spot. One that drops the word "not" can flip the meaning.

Seeing: screen readers, read-aloud and image descriptions

A [ita_term define="Software that reads out loud, or shows in braille, what is on a screen, so people can use a device without seeing it."]screen reader[/ita_term] speaks what is on the screen. It lets a person move around a page by headings, links and buttons. Screen readers themselves mostly follow fixed rules; AI shows up in newer extras, like more natural voices and automatic image descriptions.

[ita_term define="A feature that reads written text aloud in a computer voice."]Text-to-speech[/ita_term] reads any text aloud. Some apps can also describe a photo, or read the text in a picture of a sign or a letter. This can be a big help. But an automatic description can miss what matters, guess wrong about people, or misread a number.

A picture of a letter often holds a name, an address or an account number. Before you take that photo with an app, check its settings for where pictures are sent and stored.

Reading: simpler words and summaries

A chatbot can rewrite a dense letter in shorter sentences, explain a hard word, or turn a long page into a short list. This can help people with dyslexia and people learning a language. It also helps anyone who is tired, unwell or facing a wall of official text.

The risk is that a simpler version can leave out a detail or change it. For anything important, like a deadline or an amount owed, check the original. For a medicine dose, check the original and ask a pharmacist or doctor if unsure.

Typing and moving: dictation and voice control

Dictation lets you speak instead of type. Voice control goes further and lets you open apps or press buttons by voice. These help people with limited hand movement or pain, and anyone whose hands are busy. Like captions, dictation can mishear, so read the text back before you send it.

Where these features fall short

AI features are helpers. They can't fix a page or a lesson that wasn't built to be accessible in the first place.

A screen reader can only read what is there. If a document has no real headings, the screen reader user can't jump around it. If a chart has no written description, they miss it, and an automatic guess is a weak stand-in. The person who chose an image knows why it's there; an automatic description can't.

So if you make materials for others, accessible design still comes first. A teacher sharing slides or a parent group sending a newsletter can use real headings, write short image descriptions and check video captions before sharing.

[ita_why title="Why do captions get some voices wrong more often?"]

Speech recognition learns from recordings of people talking, paired with the correct text. Some accents, ages or ways of speaking may have been less common in those recordings. The system has had less practice with them, so it tends to make more mistakes.

Noise and overlapping voices make the job harder for everyone. Rare names and specialist words are hard too, because the system is more likely to pick a common word that sounds similar.

[/ita_why]

Before you rely on one

Whether you're setting something up for yourself, a student or a family member, test it first.

[ita_checklist id="accessibility-feature-check"] [ita_item]Try the feature on something you already know well, so mistakes are easy to spot.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Test it in the real setting, such as a noisy classroom or a busy bus.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Check names, numbers, dates and the word "not" in any captions or dictated text.[/ita_item] [ita_item]For important letters or forms, compare a simplified version with the original.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Ask the person who uses it what helps and what gets in the way.[/ita_item] [/ita_checklist]

Try it

Test one feature on text you already know. Pick the step 2 that suits you.

  1. Open your device's accessibility settings and turn on dictation, live captions or read-aloud.
  2. For dictation, read a short paragraph from a book aloud. For captions, play a short video with clear speech. For read-aloud, have it read a page you know.
  3. Choose a part that includes one name and one number, and check how the feature handled both.
  4. Write down each mistake and what kind it was: a name, a number or a small word like "not".
  5. Repeat in a harder setting, such as a noisier room or a faster video, and add the new mistakes to your list.
