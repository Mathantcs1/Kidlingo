If you've ever stopped before forwarding a chatbot's answer to wonder whether it was right, five minutes is often enough to check it. Check only the one or two claims that matter, against one good source.

The routine, minute by minute

You need the answer, a timer, and a way to search or the book where the fact should be. Two examples run through the steps. One is a claim that the city museum is free on the first Sunday of each month. The other is a student's claim that the plant in their science project needs full sun.

Minute 1: pick the claim

Read the answer and mark the one or two claims you'll act on or repeat to someone. For the museum, that's the free Sunday. Everything else can wait. If nothing in the answer matters that much, you can stop here.

Minute 2: make it checkable

Rewrite the claim as one short sentence with the key name, number or date: "City museum: free entry, first Sunday of each month." Short sentences like this are easy to search for. If a detail looks oddly exact, see the warning signs in "Hallucinations and how to catch them".

Minutes 3 and 4: find one good source

Look for one source that isn't a chatbot and is close to the fact. For the museum, that's the museum's own website. For the plant, it's the science textbook, the seed packet or a care guide from a garden center. To judge a source you don't know, see "AI for research and checking sources".

Asking the chatbot again doesn't count, even in a new chat. It can repeat the same mistake in the same confident tone.

Minute 5: decide and write it down

Write one word next to the claim: confirmed, different or not found. If the source says something different, use the source's version. If you found nothing, don't pass the claim on as fact. Say it's unconfirmed, or leave it out.

When five minutes isn't enough

Sometimes the first source says one thing and the second says another. Stop the timer when that happens. Disagreement means the claim needs more time, or a person who knows the subject.

Some claims always deserve more than five minutes: anything about health, medicine, money, the law or safety. For those, go to an official source or ask a qualified person, such as a pharmacist, a doctor or a lawyer. For schoolwork, ask your teacher.

Facts that change need care too. Opening times, fees and rules change, and a chatbot's answer can be out of date because of its [ita_term define="The date after which a model has no training data, so it doesn't know about later events unless the app adds newer text."]training cutoff[/ita_term]. Check the date on your source as well.

[ita_why title="Limits of a five-minute check"]

The routine checks single claims. An answer can be right in every fact and still mislead by what it leaves out. The free Sunday might need a booking in advance, and the answer might not say so.

A quick source can also be wrong or out of date. That's why the routine asks for a source close to the fact, like the museum itself. A "not found" is still a useful result, because it stops you from passing on a guess.

Try it

Pick one chatbot answer from this week that you were going to act on or share. Start a timer, run the four steps above, and note the time when you reach minute 5.

When you finish, write the result next to the claim with the name of your source, for example: "Confirmed, museum website." If the timer ran out before you found a source, start with that step the next time you check something.
