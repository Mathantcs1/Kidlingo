AI is software that learns patterns from lots of examples and uses them to make predictions: which email is spam, which word comes next, what a photo shows. That's it. It isn't a mind, it doesn't understand the world the way you do, and it can be confidently wrong.

Once you see it that way, a lot of the hype and a lot of the fear get smaller. You can use these tools well without treating them as magic or as a threat.

A working definition

"Artificial intelligence" is an umbrella term. It covers any software that does a task people used to think needed human judgement: recognising a face, translating a sentence, suggesting a route, writing a paragraph. There's no single machine called "AI". There are many different systems, built in different ways, for different jobs.

Most of the AI you meet today is [ita_term define="A way of building software where the program learns patterns from examples instead of following only rules a person wrote by hand."]machine learning[/ita_term]. Instead of a programmer writing every rule ("if the email contains these words, it's spam"), the system is shown many examples and adjusts itself until its guesses match them well.

You've probably used machine learning many times today without noticing:

  • Your email's spam filter decides which messages to hide.
  • Your phone keyboard suggests the next word as you type.
  • A map app estimates how long a trip will take.
  • A photo app groups pictures that seem to show the same person.

Chatbots are the newest and most talked-about kind. They're built on a [ita_term define="A program trained on a very large amount of text to predict which piece of text is likely to come next."]language model[/ita_term], and that's where most of the confusion starts.

How a chatbot produces an answer

When you send a message to a chatbot, it doesn't look up a stored answer. It writes one, a small piece at a time.

[ita_figure name="chatbot-answer-flow" alt="Your message is split into pieces, the model predicts the next piece, adds it to the answer, and repeats until done." caption="A chatbot builds its answer one predicted piece at a time."]

Your text is first split into [ita_term define="A small piece of text, often a whole short word or part of a longer one, that a language model reads and writes."]tokens[/ita_term]. The model then predicts which token is likely to come next, adds it, and predicts again. It repeats this until the answer is complete. The result can read like careful thought, but each step is a prediction based on patterns the model picked up during training.

This explains a lot of everyday behaviour. A chatbot is good at producing text that sounds right, because sounding right is exactly what it was trained to do. Being right is a different matter. Usually the two line up. Sometimes they don't.

[ita_why title="Why can the same question get different answers?"]

At each step the model doesn't pick one fixed next token. It produces a list of possible next tokens, each with a likelihood. The tool then picks one, usually with a bit of randomness, so the text doesn't get stiff and repetitive.

Because one early choice changes everything after it, two runs of the same prompt can drift apart. That's normal, not a fault.

The model's built-in knowledge also has an end date. It only "knows" what was in its [ita_term define="The large collection of text and other examples a model learned from before it was released."]training data[/ita_term], which stops at a cutoff. Some tools add web search results or your own files to the conversation, and the model can then use that extra text. Without that, it can't know about anything that happened after its training.

[/ita_why]

What AI isn't

A few common beliefs cause most of the trouble people run into.

It isn't a person. When a chatbot says "I think" or "I'm happy to help", it's producing words that fit the conversation. That's how people write, so that's how it learned to write. Treat it as a tool that produces text, not as a friend or an expert with opinions of its own.

It isn't a search engine. Unless the tool is connected to search, the model is answering from patterns, not from a page it just read. It can mix up names, dates and numbers, or invent a source that doesn't exist. This is often called a [ita_term define="When a model states something false or made up as if it were true, usually in a fluent and confident tone."]hallucination[/ita_term].

It isn't neutral. Models learn from text written by people, and that text carries people's blind spots and biases. The output can repeat them.

It isn't one thing. A spam filter and a chatbot are both called AI, but they work differently and fail differently. "AI says" is never a good enough reason on its own.

Where it helps, and where to be careful

Chatbots are useful for jobs where a draft or a fresh angle helps and you can judge the result yourself: rewording an email, summarising your own notes, explaining an idea in simpler words, suggesting questions to ask, or turning a messy list into a tidy one.

Be more careful with anything where a wrong answer costs you: facts you'll repeat, numbers, quotes, recent events, and anything about health, law or money. Use the answer as a starting point, then check it against a reliable source or a qualified person.

A simple habit helps a lot: ask the chatbot to show you where it might be wrong.

[ita_prompt title="Ask for an explanation and its weak spots"] Explain what machine learning is in five short sentences, for someone with no technical background. Then list two things in your explanation that I should double-check, and why. [/ita_prompt]

The second part won't catch every mistake, but it nudges you into checking instead of just accepting.

Try it

Take five minutes to see prediction, not lookup, in action.

  1. Open any AI chatbot you have access to and paste the prompt above.
  2. Read the answer and the two things it says to double-check.
  3. Start a new chat and paste the same prompt again. Compare the two answers: what stayed the same, and what changed?
  4. Pick one sentence from either answer and check it against a source you trust, such as a textbook or a well-known encyclopedia.

Notice how the wording shifts between runs while the main idea stays put. That's the prediction at work.
