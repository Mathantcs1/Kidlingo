Say you're selling handmade candles at a weekend craft fair for the first time. You ask a chatbot to "plan everything for the fair". The answer is long. It mentions pricing, a table display, card payments and packing, and goes deep on none of them.

A big task works better in pieces. Ask for the steps first. Then work on one step at a time, and check each one before you move to the next.

Step 1: Ask for the list, not the work

Your first [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] asks only for the plan:

[ita_prompt title="Steps only"] Help me prepare for [the big task, for example: my first weekend stall at a craft fair]. List the main steps from now until it's done, in order. Number them, and give each one a short name. Don't do any of the steps yet. [/ita_prompt]

Now read the list with a pen in your hand. You know things the chatbot doesn't.

  • Strike through steps you've already done.
  • Join two steps if they're really one job.
  • Add steps it missed. Maybe the fair needs a form from you by Friday.
  • Change the order if your week needs it.

Fixing the plan now takes a minute. Fixing it after the chatbot has written five pages takes much longer.

Step 2: Work on one step at a time

Pick one step, such as pricing. Give the chatbot the details for that step only. Say what the candles cost you to make, how long each takes, and what similar stalls charge. Ask for a short answer you can check.

Short answers are easier to check. If a number looks wrong, you'll see it now, while it's small. In one huge answer, a wrong price could quietly shape the sign, the stock count and the cash you bring for change.

The same approach works for school. A research presentation could be split into: choose the question, find sources, write the main points, make the slides, practice out loud. Each step gets its own message.

Step 3: Repeat what you decided

As you finish steps, keep a short list of what you've decided. Paste it at the top of each new step, so the chatbot works from your choices and doesn't invent new ones.

[ita_prompt title="Next step, with what's decided"] Decided so far: [3 to 5 short lines, for example: price per candle, table size, cash and card payments] Now help with step [number]: [step name]. Give me a short draft I can check, under 150 words. [/ita_prompt]

This list matters most in long chats. The earlier article on giving context explains that a chatbot can lose track of details from the start of a long conversation. Your list puts them back in view. It also lets you start a fresh chat for a step without losing anything.

[ita_why title="Why does one huge request go wrong?"]

Every answer has a length limit. When you ask for everything at once, the chatbot has to fit every part into one answer, so each part gets a few lines. Later parts can be rushed, or the answer can stop before the end.

There's also no chance to steer. In one big answer, the chatbot makes every choice on its own, one after another. If an early choice is wrong, the later parts build on it. Going step by step puts you back in charge of each choice.

[/ita_why]

Not every task needs this. A two-line text to a friend is fine in one message. Split a task when its parts depend on each other. Split it, too, when one long answer would be hard to check.

Try it

Do the first part on paper, before you open any app.

  1. Choose one big task from your week: a school project, a job application, a move, or an event.
  2. Write the steps you can think of, one per line.
  3. Send the "Steps only" prompt. Add any new steps it lists to your paper, in a different color.
  4. Circle the one step you'll do first.
  5. Send the second prompt for that step only, with your "Decided so far" lines filled in.
