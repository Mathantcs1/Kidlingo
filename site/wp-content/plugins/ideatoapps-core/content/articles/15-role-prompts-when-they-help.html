Does starting with "You are an expert..." make a chatbot's answer better? In a limited way, sometimes. A role changes how the answer sounds: the words, the level of detail, what it pays attention to. It doesn't give the model knowledge it lacks, and it doesn't make the facts more reliable.

What a role changes

A role is a line in a [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] that tells the chatbot whose voice or viewpoint to use. "Explain photosynthesis as an elementary school teacher would" gets shorter sentences and everyday comparisons. "Explain photosynthesis as a plant biologist would" gets more terms and more detail.

In both examples, the role also told the chatbot who was listening. Often you can get the same effect by naming the audience directly: "Explain photosynthesis for a ten-year-old."

A role can shift four things:

  • Tone: friendly, formal, blunt, playful.
  • Vocabulary: everyday words or the terms of a field.
  • Detail: a short overview or every step.
  • Focus: what gets noticed first, such as structure, evidence or mood.

Roles that are worth adding

The reader you're writing for. This role often helps most. Ask the chatbot to read your work as your audience would and say where it would lose interest. University students can use it on an essay introduction. Creators can use it on the first lines of a video script.

[ita_prompt title="Read it as your audience"] Read the text below as [describe the reader, for example: a new viewer deciding whether to keep watching]. Do not rewrite it. Mark the first sentence where this reader would lose interest, and say why in one line. Then list up to three questions this reader would still have at the end. Text: [paste your script, introduction or paragraph] [/ita_prompt]

Treat its answer as a guess about your reader rather than a measurement.

A practice partner. A debate opponent who argues the other side, or a podcast host who asks a guest hard questions. The role gives the chatbot a job in the conversation, which can keep its replies focused, especially if you also ask for short turns.

A specific kind of critic. "An editor who cares only about clarity" is more useful than "an expert". It tells the chatbot what to look for and what to ignore.

Where a role doesn't help

Facts. An "expert economist" role can still give you a wrong figure, and it may sound more certain while doing it. A confident tone is not evidence (the article "How a chatbot 'knows' things" explains why). Check figures, names and dates as you would any other answer.

Professional judgment. "Act as my doctor" or "act as my lawyer" doesn't produce a doctor's or a lawyer's judgment. It produces text in their style. For health, legal or money decisions, ask a qualified person.

Long backstories. A paragraph about the expert's career, awards and personality is mostly padding. One line that names the voice and the audience is usually enough.

[ita_why title="Why would a role change the answer at all?"]

A language model writes by predicting likely text. During training it read text written by many kinds of people: teachers, scientists, reviewers, fans. A role line makes text in that voice more likely, so the answer moves toward that style.

Whether expert roles also make answers more accurate is a debated question. Some studies report small gains on some tasks, and others find little or no effect. Results vary between models and change as models are updated, so treat any accuracy gain as unproven for your task.

Try it

Rank your readers before you ask for feedback.

  1. Pick a short piece: a university essay introduction, a caption, or the first lines of a script.
  2. Write three one-line reader roles: someone new to the topic, someone in a hurry, and someone who disagrees with you.
  3. Rank them from the reader you're most likely to lose to the one you're least likely to lose.
  4. Run the "Read it as your audience" prompt with your top-ranked reader, and fix only the sentence it marks.
