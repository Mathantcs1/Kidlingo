Checking that a source exists is only the first check. A chatbot can name a real report and still get wrong what the report says. It can also give a real article the wrong year, or link a quote to the wrong person. If you've ever found a source from a reading list and then couldn't find the quote anywhere in it, you've seen this problem.

For research, check each claim against the source itself.

Three beliefs that lead to bad citations

A [ita_term define="A reference that tells the reader where a fact or quote came from, such as an author, title and date."]citation[/ita_term] can fail even when every source in it is real. Invented references are a separate problem, and "Hallucinations and how to catch them" covers how to spot them. These three beliefs cause the quieter failures.

"If the source is real, the citation is fine."

A real source can be cited for something it doesn't say. The claim may come from a different source on the same topic, or the source may make a weaker claim than the chatbot's sentence. Details can also be mixed: the right author with the wrong year, or a title that blends two real ones.

"With search turned on, the links prove it."

Search helps, because you can open the pages the chatbot used. It doesn't guarantee a match. A cited page can still fail to support the sentence it's attached to, so the link is where your check starts.

"A news story about a study is as good as the study."

A news story is a report about the research. It may leave out the study's limits, such as a small sample or a single city, and the headline may say more than the study found. For any claim you rely on, the study itself is the source to check.

Plan the search with a chatbot

A chatbot is more useful before you have sources than after. It can suggest search words you didn't think of, the kinds of sources that would answer your question, and the questions to ask of each one. A [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] that asks for a plan, with no titles at all, keeps invented references out of your list.

You then search with those phrases yourself, through your school's or a public library's search page, or a general search engine. You've probably done this kind of searching before; the chatbot only helps you start with better words.

Teachers can use the same plan with a class. Each student runs one search phrase and finds one source. Then the class checks the sources together, using the questions from part 3 of the prompt.

Put your own research question in the brackets (example values: replace them):

[ita_prompt title="Research map"] I'm researching this question: [How do street trees affect summer temperatures in cities?] Do not name specific sources, titles or authors. Instead, give me: 1. Six search phrases, including words that experts in this field use. 2. The kinds of sources that would answer the question well, and what each kind is good for. 3. Three questions I should ask about any source I find. 4. Two points where experts may disagree, written as questions. [/ita_prompt]

Check each source

Use these checks, in this order, for each source you plan to cite.

Checks for a source before you cite it
CheckHow
It existsFind it yourself by title and author, through a library search or a search engine.
It says what's claimedFind the exact sentence, table or figure that supports the claim, and read the paragraph around it.
Who made it, and whyNote the author or organization, and whether it sells, campaigns or reports.
WhenCheck the date, and look for a newer edition or a correction.
Original or reportIf it describes a study, find the study and check the claim there.

Copy the supporting sentence and its page or section into your notes next to each claim. A claim with no supporting sentence needs another source, or you remove it from your work.

[ita_why title="Primary and secondary sources"]

A primary source is the original: the study, the data, the speech, the official report. A secondary source describes or interprets primary sources, such as a news story, a textbook or a review article.

Secondary sources are useful for an overview, but details can be lost or changed in the retelling. When a claim matters to your argument, trace it to the primary source and check it there.
