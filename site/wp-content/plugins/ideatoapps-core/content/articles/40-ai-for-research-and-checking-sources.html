Checking that a source exists is only the first check. A chatbot can name a real report and still get wrong what the report says. It can also give a real article the wrong year, or link a quote to the wrong person.

For research, check each claim against the source itself.

Three beliefs that lead to bad citations

A [ita_term define="A reference that tells the reader where a fact or quote came from, such as an author, title and date."]citation[/ita_term] can fail even when every source in it is real. Invented references are a separate problem; to spot them, see "Hallucinations and how to catch them". These three beliefs cause failures that are harder to notice.

"If the source is real, the citation is fine."

A real source can be cited for something it doesn't say. The claim may come from a different source on the same topic, or the source may make a weaker claim than the chatbot's sentence. Details can also be mixed: the right author with the wrong year, or a title that blends two real ones. If you've ever found a source from a reading list and then couldn't find the quote anywhere in it, this is a likely reason.

"With search turned on, the links prove it."

Search helps, because you can open the pages the chatbot used. It doesn't guarantee a match. A cited page can still fail to support the sentence it's attached to, so the link is where your check starts.

"A news story about a study is as good as the study."

A news story is a report about the research. It may leave out the study's limits, such as a small sample or a single city, and the headline may say more than the study found. For any claim you rely on, the study itself is the source to check.

[ita_why title="Primary and secondary sources"]

A primary source is the original: the study, the data, the speech, the official report. A secondary source describes or interprets primary sources, such as a news story, a textbook or a review article. Fields draw this line differently; in history, a news report from the time can be a primary source.

Secondary sources are useful for an overview, but details can be lost or changed each time someone retells them. When a claim matters to your argument, trace it to the primary source and check it there.

[/ita_why]

Plan the search with a chatbot

First, check your class's rules on using AI for research assignments. Where it's allowed, a chatbot is often more useful before you have sources than after. Ask it for a research map instead of a reading list. A [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] for a map gives your question, says "Do not name specific sources, titles or authors", and asks for four things:

  • six search phrases, including words that experts in the field use;
  • the kinds of sources that would answer the question well, and what each kind is good for;
  • three questions to ask about any source you find;
  • two points where experts may disagree, written as questions.

Because the map names no titles, no invented references can get into your list. You then search with those phrases yourself, through your school's or a public library's search page, or a general search engine.

Teachers can use the same map with a class. Each student runs one search phrase and finds one source. Then the class checks the sources together, using the questions from the map.

Check each source

Tick these off for each source you plan to cite, in this order:

[ita_checklist id="source-checks-before-citing"] [ita_item]It exists: you found it yourself by title and author, through a library search or a search engine.[/ita_item] [ita_item]It says what's claimed: you found the exact sentence, table or figure that supports the claim, and read the paragraph around it.[/ita_item] [ita_item]You know who made it, and whether they are selling something, arguing for a cause or reporting.[/ita_item] [ita_item]You checked the date, and looked for a newer edition, a correction or a retraction.[/ita_item] [ita_item]If it describes a study, you found the study and checked the claim there.[/ita_item] [/ita_checklist]

Copy the supporting sentence and its page or section into your notes next to each claim, and quote or reword it with a citation when you use it. If you can't find a supporting sentence, find another source or remove the claim.

Try it

Pick one research question for a current assignment, or for a lesson you'll teach. Ask a chatbot for a research map as described above, use one of its search phrases to find a source yourself, and copy the exact sentence that supports one claim you plan to use, with the page or section number.
