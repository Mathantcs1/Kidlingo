It's Sunday evening, and you need a 45-minute lesson on equivalent fractions for Tuesday. A chatbot can draft a plan from a short [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] in a minute. It doesn't know your students, what they did last week or which activities work in your room. You bring that knowledge, and you make the final decisions. If you've ever planned a lesson at the kitchen table, you know how much of the work is fitting the pieces into the time you have.

A first request, and a better one

Here is a typical first request: "Write a lesson plan on equivalent fractions for grade 5." It works, but the chatbot has to fill every gap with a typical choice. The draft may run longer than your period, use materials you don't have and ignore what your class did last week.

The better request starts with the conditions the plan must fit. Before you write it, check your school's or district's rules on AI tools and student information. Then describe the class in general terms: grade, subject, goal, time, the previous lesson, materials and the group. Describe needs without names, such as "some students read below grade level" or "three are learning English". Leave out names, grades, health plans and anything else that could identify a student.

[ita_prompt title="Fixed conditions, then a timed plan"] Fixed conditions for one lesson: Grade and subject: [grade 5 math] Goal: students can [find two fractions equal to a given fraction] Time: [45 minutes] Previous lesson: [students used fraction strips to compare halves and quarters] Materials: [fraction strips, mini whiteboards] Group: [26 students; some read below grade level; three are learning English] Write a lesson plan with a time for each part. The times must add up to [45] minutes. For each part, say what students do, not only what the teacher says. Add one quick check for understanding before independent practice. List two places where students are likely to get confused, and what the teacher could say. [/ita_prompt]

Where your judgment comes in

You've probably adapted someone else's lesson plan before, and this is the same job. Read the draft as you would a plan from a new colleague, and check these points before you use any of it:

  • Timing. Drafts can fit more into a lesson than a real class can do. Cut activities rather than rushing them.
  • Accuracy. Work every example yourself. A chatbot can make a mistake in a worked fraction problem just as it can in a fact.
  • Your students. Swap examples for ones your class will recognize, and adjust the pace for the group you know.
  • Materials and safety. Remove anything you don't have. For practical work, follow your school's safety rules, whatever the draft says.
  • Curriculum links. Match the plan to your own curriculum documents.

When one activity is too hard for part of the group, ask for that activity in three versions: with more support, as written, and with an extra challenge. Then choose one, or mix parts of them.

The same method works outside school. A tutor running a weekly class for older adults at a community center could describe the group, the time and a goal such as "can join a video call from a phone". The draft would then need checking against what this group already knows and the pace it prefers.

[ita_why title="Standard codes and curriculum references"]

Ask a chatbot to link a plan to standards and it may list codes and wording that look official. Some of them may come from another region or an older version, or may not exist at all. The model has seen many standards documents and can produce text in their shape.

If your school expects standard codes on plans, copy them from the official document yourself. The chatbot's suggestions are useful only as a hint about which area to look in.

Try it

This one works with a colleague who teaches the same grade or subject.

  1. Pick a lesson you'll teach in the next two weeks.
  2. Fill in the fixed conditions with your real class, describing needs without names, and get a draft. Use an app your school allows.
  3. Mark each part of the draft: keep, change or cut.
  4. Give the marked draft to your colleague and ask them to circle the one part they would change.
  5. Rewrite that part yourself before you teach the lesson.
