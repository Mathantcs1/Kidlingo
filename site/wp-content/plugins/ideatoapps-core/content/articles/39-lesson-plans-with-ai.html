It's Sunday evening, and you need a 45-minute lesson on equivalent fractions for Tuesday. A chatbot can draft a plan from a short [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] in a minute. It doesn't know your students, what they did last week or which activities work in your room, so the final decisions stay with you.

A first request, and a better one

Here is a typical first request: "Write a lesson plan on equivalent fractions for grade 5." It works, but the chatbot has to guess everything you left out, and it guesses the most typical answer. The draft may run longer than your period, use materials you don't have and ignore what your class did last week.

The better request starts with the conditions the plan must fit. Before you write it, check your school's or district's rules on AI tools and student information. Then describe the class in general terms, with needs instead of names: "some students read below grade level", "three are learning English". Leave out names, test scores, health plans and anything else that could identify a student. In a small class, even a very specific detail can.

Put these lines at the top of your request, with your own class's details, then ask for the plan:

[ita_checklist id="lesson-plan-request"] [ita_item]Grade and subject, for example grade 5 math.[/ita_item] [ita_item]The goal in your own words, for example "students can find two fractions equal to a given fraction".[/ita_item] [ita_item]The time you have, and what students did in the previous lesson.[/ita_item] [ita_item]The materials and space you have.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Group size and needs, described without names.[/ita_item] [ita_item]A time for each part, with the times adding up to the length of the lesson.[/ita_item] [ita_item]What students do in each part, as well as what the teacher says.[/ita_item] [ita_item]A quick check for understanding before independent practice, and a short check of what students learned at the end.[/ita_item] [ita_item]Two places where students are likely to get confused, and what the teacher could say.[/ita_item] [/ita_checklist]

Where your judgment comes in

Read the draft as you would a plan from a new colleague who has never met your class. Check these points before you use any of it:

  • Timing. Drafts can fit more into a lesson than a real class can do. Cut activities rather than rushing them.
  • Accuracy. Work every example yourself. A chatbot can get a worked fraction problem wrong even when the method looks right.
  • Your students. Swap examples for ones your class will recognize, and adjust the pace for the group you know.
  • Materials and safety. Remove anything you don't have. For practical work, follow your school's safety rules, whatever the draft says.
  • Curriculum links. Match the plan to your own curriculum documents.
[ita_why title="Standard codes and curriculum references"]

Ask a chatbot to link a plan to standards and it may list codes and wording that look official. Some of them may come from another region or an older version, or may not exist at all. The model has seen many standards documents and can produce text in their shape.

If your school expects standard codes on plans, copy them from the official document yourself. The chatbot's suggestions are useful only as a hint about which area to look in.

[/ita_why]

When one activity is too hard for part of the group, ask for that activity in three versions: with more support, as written, and with an extra challenge. Then choose one, or mix parts of them.

The same method works outside school. A tutor running a weekly class for older adults at a community center could describe the group, the time and a goal such as "can join a video call from a phone". Check the draft against what this group already knows, and slow the pace if they need more time.

Try it

This one works with a colleague who teaches the same grade or subject.

  1. Pick a lesson you'll teach in the next two weeks.
  2. Write the lines from the list above for your real class, describing needs without names, and get a draft. Use an app your school allows.
  3. Mark each part of the draft: keep, change or cut.
  4. Give the marked draft to your colleague and ask them to circle the one part they would change.
  5. Rewrite that part yourself before you teach the lesson.
