A common mistake with long documents is to paste the whole thing and ask one broad question, such as "what are the risks here?" The document may fit in the model's context window, and the answer can still miss the one paragraph that matters.

The earlier article on giving context showed what happens in a long chat: the oldest messages can be dropped. This one looks at what fills the window, and at how to use that space on purpose.

What fills the window

The [ita_term define="The amount of text, counted in tokens, that a model can take into account at one time."]context window[/ita_term] is everything the model can take into account when it writes the next part of its answer. Your messages are only part of it. Depending on the app, it can also hold:

  • the app's own instructions, which you usually can't see;
  • saved memory notes about you;
  • files, pages or documents you added;
  • search results and the results of any tools the app used;
  • every earlier reply from the chatbot;
  • room for the reply it's writing now, since in most setups the reply counts toward the same limit.

All of it is measured in tokens, the small chunks of text covered in the article on tokens. A long search result or a pasted spreadsheet can take more space than a whole conversation.

Fitting in is not the same as being read well

Context windows have grown, and many apps now accept very long documents. A bigger window holds more text, but the model doesn't weigh every part of it equally.

Some research has found that models use details in the middle of a long input less reliably than details near the start or the end. Results vary between models and keep changing, so treat this as a risk to check rather than a fixed rule.

For work documents, such as a staff handbook, a contract you're reviewing with your manager, or a supplier's terms, a few habits help:

  • Point the model at the part that matters, by section name or heading.
  • Ask one focused question at a time.
  • Ask it to quote the sentence its answer is based on, so you can find that sentence in the original.
[ita_prompt title="Point it to the right part"] The document below is our [type of document, for example: staff handbook]. Use only the section called [section name] to answer. Question: [your question] First quote the sentence or sentences your answer is based on. Then give the answer in plain words. If that section does not answer the question, say so. Document: [paste the document, or only that section] [/ita_prompt]

Checking the quote against the original document takes seconds. If the sentence isn't there, the answer isn't safe to use.

For builders: managing the budget

In your own app, you decide what goes into the window on every request. Treat the space as a budget:

  • Reserve room for the reply. Count input tokens before sending, and leave enough space for the longest answer you expect.
  • Trim the history. Keep the system instructions and the last few turns whole. Replace older turns with a short state note.
  • Fetch, don't stuff. Instead of sending a whole knowledge base, look up the few passages that match the question and send those.
  • Shrink tool results. A tool may return far more text than the model needs. Keep only the fields that matter before passing it back.
  • Leave space unused when you can. Larger requests are usually slower and use more computing, and extra text gives the model more chances to go wrong.

A state note can come from the model itself. Run a [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] like this on older turns, and send its output in place of them:

[ita_prompt title="Rolling state note"] Summarize the conversation below as a state note for the next turn. Use exactly these headings, with short lines: GOAL: 1 line DECISIONS: up to 5 lines FACTS FROM USER: up to 8 lines; copy numbers, dates and names exactly OPEN QUESTIONS: up to 3 lines Leave out greetings, repeated text and anything already marked done. Conversation: [older turns] [/ita_prompt] [ita_why title="What does a summary lose?"]

A summary saves space by dropping detail. Anything that doesn't fit one of the headings is gone from the window, even if it matters three turns later. A short remark such as "I can't do mornings" can disappear this way.

Errors travel too. If the summary gets a date wrong, every later turn treats the wrong date as a fact. Two habits reduce the damage. Store the full conversation outside the window, so you can fetch it when needed. Test the summary prompt on real conversations before you rely on it.

Try it

Make a one-page "what goes in" list that a teammate could use.

  1. Pick one long document you use at work, or one feature of an app you build.
  2. List everything that will sit in the window when the model answers: instructions, the document or passages, history, tool results, and the reply.
  3. Mark each item must keep, can summarize, or can drop.
  4. Test one item. Workers: run "Point it to the right part" on one section and find the quoted sentence in the document. Builders: run the state note on a long test conversation and check every number and date.
  5. Give the list to the person who will use the document or maintain the feature next.
