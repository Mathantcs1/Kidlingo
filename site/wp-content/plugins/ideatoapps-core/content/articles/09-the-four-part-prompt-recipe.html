A good prompt answers four questions: what you want done, what the AI needs to know, what the answer should look like, and what to avoid. That's the whole recipe: Task, Context, Format, Limits.

You don't need special words or tricks. You need to say the things a helpful stranger would ask you before starting the job.

The recipe at a glance

[ita_figure name="prompt-recipe" alt="Four numbered parts of a prompt: task, context, format, and limits such as length and tone." caption="Task, Context, Format, Limits. Add the parts that matter."]

Here's what each part means in practice:

  • Task: the job, in one clear sentence that starts with a verb. "Write", "summarise", "compare", "suggest", "explain".
  • Context: the background only you know. Who it's for, what's already happened, what you've got to work with.
  • Format: the shape of the answer. A list, a table, an email with a subject line, three options to choose from.
  • Limits: length, tone, reading level, and anything to leave out.

The names aren't an official standard. They're just an easy way to remember what a chatbot can't guess on its own.

Weak prompt, better prompt

Say your heater has stopped working and you want to email your landlord. Here's the kind of prompt most people type first:

[ita_prompt title="Weak version"] Write an email about my broken heater. [/ita_prompt]

This will work, after a fashion. You'll get a polite, generic email. But the chatbot has to guess everything else: who you're writing to, how long the heater has been broken, whether this is the first time you've asked, and how formal to be. It fills each gap with the most typical choice, which is why the result sounds like a template.

Now the same request with all four parts:

[ita_prompt title="Better version"] Task: Write an email to my landlord asking for the heater in my flat to be repaired. Context: The heater stopped working three days ago. I reported it by phone on the first day and nothing has happened yet. The nights are getting cold. The landlord is usually friendly but slow to reply. Format: A subject line, then three short paragraphs: what's wrong, what I've already done, and what I'm asking for. Limits: Under 150 words. Polite but firm. Ask for a repair date, not an apology. No threats. [/ita_prompt]

The second prompt takes about a minute longer to write. In return, the draft is usually much closer to something you'd actually send, so you spend less time editing.

Each part, a little deeper

Task: one job at a time

If you ask for three different things in one breath, the answer often does all three half-well. Pick the main job. You can ask for the next one in a follow-up message.

Context: what only you know

The chatbot only knows about your situation what's in the conversation: your messages, plus any files or saved memory the tool adds. It doesn't know your landlord, your deadline or your reader. Two or three sentences of background often make a big difference.

Format: describe the shape

If you want a table, say so, and name the columns. If you want a checklist you can print, say that. Naming the shape you want is far more useful than asking for "a good answer".

Limits: length, tone, and no-go areas

Limits stop the answer from wandering. Note that length limits are followed only roughly. Ask for 150 words and you may get a bit more or a bit less, so count if it really matters.

[ita_why title="Why does context matter so much?"]

A chatbot writes its answer by predicting likely text, one piece at a time. With a short prompt, many different answers are equally likely, so it drifts toward the most common, middle-of-the-road version.

Every detail you add narrows the options. "Email to my landlord" rules out a lot. "Reported by phone three days ago" rules out more. By the end, the most likely answer is one that fits your situation, not anyone's.

That's also why the order of the four parts doesn't matter much. What matters is that the information is there. Labels like Task: and Context: simply make it easier for you to see what's missing.

[/ita_why]

You won't need all four parts every time. "Translate this sentence into Spanish" is a complete task. Add the other parts when the first answer comes back too generic, too long, or the wrong shape.

Try it

Pick a small real task you have this week: a message to a neighbour, a shopping list for a recipe, or a short plan for revising one topic.

  1. Write the weak version first, in one line, and send it to any chatbot you use.
  2. Now write the four parts on separate lines: Task, Context, Format, Limits. Keep each one short.
  3. Send the better version in a new chat.
  4. Put the two answers side by side and note one thing that improved and one thing you would still change.

If the second answer still misses, don't start over. Add the missing detail to the part it belongs to and send it again.
