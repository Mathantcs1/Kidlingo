Two students, both made up for this example, are stuck on the same chemistry question. One pastes it into a chatbot and copies the answer. The other asks for a hint, tries again, and then explains the method in their own words. Both finish the homework, but only the second one practiced what the test will ask.

Ask for hints instead of answers

You may have read a worked answer, agreed with every step, and then got stuck on the same problem in a test. Following an answer and doing it yourself are different skills. A chatbot will usually solve the whole problem if your [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] lets it. So tell it not to, and ask for one small hint at a time.

Sometimes it gives the answer anyway. If that happens, write "Hints only, please" in your next message and try the problem again yourself.

Chatbots can also make mistakes in step-by-step work, including math and chemistry. When you reach an answer, check it against your textbook, the answer key or a worked example from class.

Here is a version to copy. Put your own subject and problem in the brackets:

[ita_prompt title="Hints, one at a time"] I'm studying [chemistry: balancing equations]. Here is a problem I'm stuck on: [paste the problem] Do not give me the answer. Give me one hint, then wait for my next try. If my next try is wrong, give a smaller hint about my first wrong step only. Give the full worked answer only if I write SHOW ME. [/ita_prompt]

Explain it back

Hints help with one problem. To check whether you understand a whole topic, switch roles: you explain, and the chatbot listens. Explaining something to a friend often shows you, halfway through, that you weren't sure of it. Explaining to a chatbot uses that same effect on purpose.

Close your notes and write a few sentences explaining the topic, as if to a classmate who missed the lesson. Paste them in and ask: "What is missing or wrong in my explanation? Don't rewrite it. Ask me one question about the weakest part." Answer that question in writing, still without your notes. Then open your notes and check the part it questioned.

A university student in a statistics course could explain when to use the median instead of the mean. A high school student could explain why ice floats on water. Either way, the gaps appear in your own words, where you can fix them.

The chatbot's feedback can be wrong too. If it says your explanation is wrong, check your textbook or class materials, and ask your teacher.

[ita_why title="Explaining and remembering"]

People who study learning often find that recalling information from memory helps you keep it longer than reading it again. Explaining a topic in your own words is one way to do that. It also shows you what you can't explain yet.

How much it helps depends on the subject and the person. Rereading feels easier, which is part of why it's popular. When you review, close the book and try to recall the main points first, then check.

[/ita_why]

Check your rules first

Schools, teachers and courses have different rules about AI. One class may allow it for practice but not for anything graded. Another may ask you to say how you used it. A university course may have its own rule in the syllabus.

Graded work includes essays, lab reports and take-home tests. Before you use a chatbot for any of it, check your school's rules or ask your teacher. If the rules aren't clear, ask before you start.

Check the app's age rules too. Many AI apps set a minimum age, and some need a parent's permission for younger users. For what to leave out of any chat, see "What not to paste into an AI tool".
