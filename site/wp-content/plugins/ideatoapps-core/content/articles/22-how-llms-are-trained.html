A large language model is trained in two broad phases. First, it learns to predict the next piece of text (a token) across a huge collection of writing. Then it's trained further to act as a helpful assistant, using example conversations and people's judgments of its answers.

Most of what a chatbot does well, and many of its mistakes, come from one of these two phases.

Pretraining: guessing the next token

The first phase is called [ita_term define="The first and largest training phase, in which a model learns to predict the next token of ordinary text."]pretraining[/ita_term]. It starts with collecting text: public web pages, books, code and other sources. Developers usually filter it, for example to remove duplicate pages and low-quality text. The exact mix is often not published.

The text is split into tokens. Then the training loop runs, again and again:

  1. The model sees a stretch of real text with the next token hidden.
  2. It gives a likelihood to every token that could come next.
  3. Its guess is compared with the real next token.
  4. Its [ita_term define="The very large set of numbers inside a model that training adjusts."]parameters[/ita_term] are nudged slightly, so the real token becomes a little more likely next time.

Repeated across an enormous amount of text, this loop builds grammar, facts, styles and common patterns of reasoning. It often runs on large groups of specialized chips for weeks or months.

The result is a [ita_term define="A model that has only been pretrained, so it continues text instead of following instructions."]base model[/ita_term]. It continues text well, but it doesn't reliably answer you. Give it "What is the capital of France?" and it might continue with more quiz questions. Lists of questions are common in its training text.

Post-training: becoming an assistant

The second phase shapes the base model into something you can talk to. It usually has two parts.

Learning from example conversations

The model is trained on many example exchanges: a request, then a good reply. People write or check many of them. From these, the model learns the shape of a helpful answer, how to follow instructions and when to decline a request. This is a form of [ita_term define="Training an existing model further on a smaller, focused set of examples."]fine-tuning[/ita_term].

Learning from preferences

Next, the model writes two or more answers to the same request, and people pick the one they prefer. Those choices are used to push the model toward answers people rate higher. The Why box below shows one common way this works.

Developers also train for safety in this phase, for example to refuse some harmful requests. Methods differ between developers and keep changing, so treat any single description as a simplified one.

[ita_why title="How people's choices become training"]

One common method first trains a separate [ita_term define="A model trained to predict how highly people would rate an answer."]reward model[/ita_term]. It learns from people's comparisons to give any answer a score that predicts how much people would like it.

The main model then writes answers, the reward model scores them, and the main model is tuned toward higher scores. Other methods skip the reward model and learn from the comparisons directly. Some developers also use a list of written rules, with feedback produced by other AI models.

[/ita_why]

What this means when you use or build with a model

Training happens before release. Your chats don't change the model's parameters. Some apps can save notes about you, but that adds text to later chats; it isn't training. Whether your chats are used to train later models depends on the provider and your settings, so check the privacy settings.

For builders, the main choice is between fine-tuning a model on your own examples and sending the right information with each request. Fine-tuning tends to work better for a fixed format, a tone or one narrow task. For facts that change, sending the current information is usually easier to update.

Students can use the two phases as a study frame. A [ita_term define="The message or instructions you type to an AI app to tell it what you want."]prompt[/ita_term] like this turns them into a short self-test:

[ita_prompt title="Quiz me on one phase"] Topic: how large language models are trained. Explain [one phase, for example: pretraining] in five short sentences for a [your level, for example: first-year university] student (example values: replace them). Then ask me three questions about it, one at a time. Wait for my answer before you ask the next question. After each answer, tell me what I got right and what I missed. [/ita_prompt]

Check the explanation against your course notes or textbook before you learn from it.
